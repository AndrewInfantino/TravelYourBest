    The trip

    Baobabs, lemurs & the long road west.

    We visited Madagascar in September 2026, flying in from the Seychelles with Emirates to the capital, Antananarivo. This incredible country has so much to see, so our honest advice is to spend as much time as you can and to have a very clear plan or book a well-organised tour. We did it in a tight five days: a private driver took us 18 hours across the island to Morondava for two jam-packed days of baobabs, lemurs and a fishing village by boat, then 18 hours back. To some that might sound rushed, but for us the timing was perfect, and we got to see the two great symbols of Madagascar, the baobab trees and the lemurs, up close.

    When to go: September was great. It's the cooler, dry winter season. We heard the summer months get very hot, and the rainy season runs roughly from December to May.

    Part one

    The road west

    Antananarivo sits high in the central highlands. From there, one long highway runs all the way across the island to Morondava on the west coast, through highland towns, red-earth hills and village after village.

    The journey01

    18 hours across Madagascar

    📍 Antananarivo → Morondava

    We did Madagascar in a very different way. We booked a five-day trip with a private driver and drove over 18 hours from the capital, Antananarivo, all the way west to Morondava, then another 18 hours back. It was extremely long, but honestly it was one of the most incredible parts of the trip. Our driver was super friendly and had an older 4x4 that, luckily for us, ran perfectly the whole way. We booked it all through Remote River Expeditions: communication was great, they built exactly the trip we wanted in the time we had, and at about €65 a day for the driver the price was very reasonable compared to the other operators we contacted.

    Eye-opening02

    Life along the road

    📍 Village to village, east to west

    The single highway that crosses the island from east to west passes through some of the most remote villages in the world, one after another. We saw children walking along what seemed like an endless road, villagers washing their clothes in the rivers, and farmers preparing their crops. It was truly eye-opening, and something we wouldn't change, even for all the hours and the distance. If you'd rather have a guide handle everything, there are longer multi-day tours that cover this route too.

    Part two

    Morondava & the baobabs

    Morondava is the gateway to Madagascar's most famous sight. We packed two very full days in here: the baobabs at sunrise and sunset, two lemur parks, and a boat trip through the mangroves to a fishing village.

    The highlight03

    The Avenue of the Baobabs

    📍 Allée des Baobabs, near Morondava

    This was the absolute highlight for us, and the whole reason we took that long drive. To us, the baobabs are the symbol of Madagascar, and they're something to truly marvel at. Their size is incredible, and walking down the Allée des Baobabs, the famous sandy road lined with these giants, felt like a miracle. We're so grateful we got to see it. If you'd like a guided version of this exact trip, this tour is a good one to compare.

    Golden hour04

    Baobabs at sunrise & sunset

    📍 The Allée des Baobabs

    We saw the Avenue of the Baobabs at both sunrise and sunset, and both were miraculous. At sunset the trees turn into perfect silhouettes against an orange and pink sky; stay a little longer and the blue hour brings out the first stars above them. At sunrise the trunks glow soft gold and it's much quieter. If you can, do both.

    Do bothSunrise is quieter
    Up close05

    Life around the baobabs

    📍 The villages beside the Avenue

    The Avenue isn't a museum. It's a working road, and life goes on around it. Zebu carts roll slowly past the trees, painted in bright colours, and small paths lead off into the bush to baobabs you can walk right up to. Standing at the base of one really shows you how small you are.

    Watch for zebu cartsWalk the side paths
    For couples06

    The Baobab Amoureux

    📍 Near Morondava

    A short drive from the Avenue are the Baobabs Amoureux, the "lovers' baobabs": two trunks that have grown twisted around each other. It's a sweet, quieter stop, and a lovely one for couples.

    Part three

    The lemurs

    Found nowhere else on Earth, lemurs are Madagascar's other great symbol. We visited two places near Morondava, and both were unbelievable.

    Up close07

    Lemurs at Kimony Resort

    📍 Kimony, near Morondava

    You can't do Madagascar without seeing lemurs, and we were so fortunate to visit two places near Morondava that were unbelievable. At Kimony Resort we had a great visit where the lemurs actually jumped onto our shoulders: ring-tailed lemurs, red-fronted brown lemurs and even a crowned lemur, all around the gardens. There were also radiated tortoises, with beautiful star-patterned shells, found only in Madagascar.

    Lemurs on your shouldersRadiated tortoises
    A forest walk08

    A guided walk at Akiba Lodge

    📍 Akiba Lodge, near Morondava

    Our second lemur visit was at Akiba Lodge, where a really nice guide walked us through the open forest to see lemurs in the wild. The white sifakas gave us quite a show, coming very close and then hopping sideways across the ground, which honestly looked like they were dancing. We even spotted a mother carrying her baby. For more forest and the famous stone "tsingy", this tour is worth comparing.

    Part four

    Betania fishing village

    Just off the coast from Morondava, reached by boat through the mangroves, is a very different side of Madagascar: a small island community that lives almost entirely from the sea.

    Eye-opening09

    A fishing village by boat

    📍 Betania, near Morondava

    We also took a tour by boat through the mangroves to the island of Betania, where a local walked us through what life is like for the village. Only around 2,000 people live there, and roughly 90% of them are fishermen. We watched boats coming in, nets being carried up the beach and children playing on the brightly painted pirogues. Most of these villages have no electricity or running water, and it was a completely eye-opening experience. Go with a local guide and be respectful: ask before taking photos of people.
